# Protein Language Models: From ProtVec to Contextualized Protein Embeddings

These slides explain how machine learning learns useful representations of protein sequences, starting with older methods such as ProtVec and n-gram models, and leading toward modern protein language models (pLMs), such as ESM and ProtT5.

The central progression is:

Raw sequence

Handcrafted features

ProtVec / Word2Vec

Context-independent embeddings

Contextualized embeddings

Modern protein language models

We will cover every concept in your slides, including the biological reason context matters, the skip-gram architecture, n-gram language models, and why unlabeled protein sequences are so valuable.

## 1. Why learn representations from unlabeled protein sequences?

Your first slide says:

> Learn representations by training on large datasets of unlabeled protein sequences.

### 1.1 What is a representation?

A representation is a numerical description of an object that a machine learning model can process.

A protein sequence is initially a string:

```
MKTLLVAGTAA...
```

A neural network cannot directly perform mathematical operations on letters. We convert the sequence into vectors.

For example:

Protein sequence⟶Numerical representation⟶ML model⟶Prediction\text{Protein sequence} \longrightarrow \text{Numerical representation} \longrightarrow \text{ML model} \longrightarrow \text{Prediction}Protein sequence⟶Numerical representation⟶ML model⟶Prediction

The representation may capture information about:

* Amino acid relationships

* Sequence patterns

* Structural tendencies

* Protein families

* Functional motifs

* Localization

* Interaction-related properties

The important point is that a good representation makes downstream biological tasks easier.

### 1.2 Why use unlabeled data?

Labeled biological data are expensive and limited.

For example, to train a model for kinase–substrate association, you may need experimentally validated associations. Obtaining those labels requires laboratory experiments, curated databases, and substantial effort.

In contrast, protein sequences are abundant. We can download millions of sequences without knowing the function of every protein.

# 2. ProtVec: Protein Word2Vec

ProtVec was introduced by Asgari and Mofrad in 2015 in the paper Continuous Distributed Representation of Biological Sequences for Deep Proteomics and Genomics.

The main idea is to apply the intuition behind Word2Vec to protein sequences.

## 2.1 Word2Vec in natural language

Consider the sentence:

```
The cat drinks milk.
The cat eats fish.
The dog drinks milk.
```

Word2Vec learns a vector for each word.

Conceptually:

cat→vcat\text{cat} \rightarrow \mathbf{v}_{cat}cat→vcat

dog→vdog\text{dog} \rightarrow \mathbf{v}_{dog}dog→vdog

milk→vmilk\text{milk} \rightarrow \mathbf{v}_{milk}milk→vmilk

Words occurring in similar contexts tend to receive similar vector representations.

For example, cat and dog often occur near words such as eats, drinks, and animal. Their vectors may therefore become similar.

The model is not explicitly told that cats and dogs are animals. It learns patterns from their contexts.

### Distributional hypothesis

The underlying linguistic idea is:

> Words used in similar contexts tend to have related meanings.

For protein sequences, we can adapt this idea:

> Amino acid patterns occurring in similar sequence contexts may have related biological or structural properties.

This analogy is useful, but proteins are not exactly natural language. Their “grammar” is determined by biochemical constraints, evolution, structure, and function.

## 3.1 Why use 3-grams instead of individual amino acids?

An individual amino acid contains limited local sequence information.

Compare:

```

```

with:

```

```

The 3-gram includes local relationships between neighboring amino acids. Those relationships may reflect:

* Hydrophobic segments

* Binding motifs

* Local structural tendencies

* Evolutionarily conserved patterns

* Cleavage or modification signals

However, a 3-gram is still a fixed local pattern. It does not fully represent long-range relationships in the protein.

# 4. ProtVec and the skip-gram architecture

Your slides mention the skip-gram architecture.

Skip-gram is one of the original Word2Vec training approaches.

## 4.1 Core idea

Given a central word, the model tries to predict words occurring around it.

For natural language:

```
The cat drinks milk
```

If the center word is:

```
cat
```

the model may try to predict neighboring words such as:

```
The
drinks
```

For proteins, the central “word” is a 3-gram, and the surrounding words are neighboring 3-grams.

Example:

```
MKT KTL TLL LLV LVA VAG
```

If the center 3-gram is `TLL`, the model might use it to predict nearby 3-grams such as `KTL` and `LLV`.

The training objective encourages representations of 3-grams that appear in similar contexts to become related.

## 4.2 Simplified architecture

### 100-dimensional vector

vTLL∈R100\mathbf{v}_{TLL}\in\mathbb{R}^{100}vTLL∈R100

Prediction

### Neighboring 3-grams

The slide states that each 3-gram receives a 100-dimensional vector.

Thus:

TLL⟶vTLL=\(v1,v2,…,v100\)\text{TLL} \longrightarrow \mathbf{v}_{TLL} = \(v\_1,v\_2,\\ldots,v\_{100}\)TLL⟶vTLL=\(v1​,v2​,…,v100​\)

The individual dimensions generally do not have predefined meanings such as “hydrophobicity” or “charge.” The vector is learned from the training objective.

### Important clarification

The model learns an embedding for a 3-gram, not necessarily a context-dependent representation of every occurrence of that 3-gram.

Therefore, if `TLL` appears in two different proteins, the basic ProtVec lookup gives the same vector:

E(TLL)=vTLLE(\text{TLL})=\mathbf{v}_{TLL}E(TLL)=vTLL

This is a major difference from modern contextualized protein language models.


# 5. What does the skip-gram model actually learn?

Let's go deeper into the mathematics.

Suppose we have a sequence of 3-grams:

```
MKT KTL TLL LLV LVA VAG AGT
```

For a central 3-gram xtx_txt, we want to predict neighboring 3-grams xt+jx_{t+j}xt+j.

The skip-gram objective can be expressed as:

max⁡θ∑t=1T∑−c≤j≤cj≠0log⁡P(xt+j∣xt)\max_{\theta} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \sum_{\substack{-c\leq j\leq c\\j\neq0}} \log P(x_{t+j}\mid x_t)θmaxt=1∑T−c≤j≤cj=0∑logP(xt+j∣xt)

Where:

* TTT: number of 3-grams in the training sequence

* ccc: context-window size

* xtx_txt: center 3-gram

* xt+jx_{t+j}xt+j: neighboring 3-gram

* P(xt+j∣xt)P(x_{t+j}\mid x_t)P(xt+j∣xt): probability of the neighboring 3-gram given the center

The model typically calculates probabilities using a softmax:

P(o∣i)=exp⁡(uo⊤vi)∑w∈Vexp⁡(uw⊤vi)P(o\mid i) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{u}_o^\top\mathbf{v}_i)} {\sum_{w\in V}\exp(\mathbf{u}_w^\top\mathbf{v}_i)}P(o∣i)=∑w∈Vexp(uw⊤vi)exp(uo⊤vi)

Here:

* vi\mathbf{v}_ivi is the input embedding of the center 3-gram.

* uo\mathbf{u}_ouo is the output vector of a candidate neighboring 3-gram.

* VVV is the vocabulary of possible 3-grams.

The model increases the similarity between center and actual neighboring 3-grams.

### What is the learning signal?

Suppose the sequence frequently contains:

```
LLV LVA VAG
```

The model learns that `LLV`, `LVA`, and `VAG` occur in related contexts.

This does not automatically mean that it has learned a specific biological mechanism. It has learned statistical regularities that may correlate with biological properties.

That distinction is important in computational biology:

> A representation can be statistically useful without every dimension having a clear biological interpretation.

# 6. Why are the embeddings 100-dimensional?

Your slide says:

> A 100 dimensional vector for each 3-gram.

A 100-dimensional embedding means:

v3gram∈R100\mathbf{v}_{3gram}\in\mathbb{R}^{100}v3gram∈R100

For example:

```
TLL → [0.12, -0.45, 0.08, ..., 0.31]
```

The number 100 is a hyperparameter, not a biological constant.

You could theoretically use:

* 10 dimensions

* 50 dimensions

* 100 dimensions

* 300 dimensions

* 1024 dimensions

Higher dimensions can store more information, but they may also increase computational cost and the risk of learning noise, especially with limited training data.

The embedding dimension should be selected based on the dataset, task, and computational resources.

## 6.1 How do we represent an entire protein?

ProtVec produces vectors for local 3-grams. To represent an entire protein, you need an additional aggregation step.

For example, if the protein contains TTT 3-grams:

v1,v2,…,vT\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2,\ldots,\mathbf{v}_Tv1,v2,…,vT

You could calculate mean pooling:

p=1T∑t=1Tvt\mathbf{p} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{v}_tp=T1t=1∑Tvt

The result is one 100-dimensional vector for the entire protein.

However, mean pooling can lose:

* The order of 3-grams

* The exact location of motifs

* Long-range relationships

* Differences between multiple occurrences

Some ProtVec implementations represent sequences through concatenated or aggregated subword vectors. The exact sequence-level representation depends on the implementation.

# 7. The limitation of basic ProtVec: context-independent embeddings

This is the key transition to your next slides.

In traditional Word2Vec, a word generally receives one fixed vector.

For example:

E(bank)=vbankE(\text{bank})=\mathbf{v}_{bank}E(bank)=vbank

But the word bank can have different meanings:

```
I deposited money in the bank.
```

versus:

```
I sat beside the river bank.
```

A basic Word2Vec model assigns the same embedding to `bank` in both sentences.

The embedding may capture information related to both meanings, but it does not dynamically change according to the sentence.

The same limitation applies to basic ProtVec.

If the 3-gram `ALV` occurs in different proteins, the basic lookup gives:

E(ALV)=vALVE(\text{ALV})=\mathbf{v}_{ALV}E(ALV)=vALV

regardless of the broader protein sequence.

This is called a context-independent representation.

# 10. What does “context” mean in a protein?

Context can exist at several levels.

1. Immediate sequence context

   The amino acids directly adjacent to the target residue.

   ```
   A L V L L V V V
       ↑
   ```

2. Local motif context

   A short pattern that may be associated with a biological function, such as a binding or modification motif.

3. Long-range sequence context

   Residues far away in the primary sequence can come close together after the protein folds.

4. Protein-level context

   The entire sequence, including domains, signal peptides, transmembrane regions, and other structural or functional patterns.

5. Evolutionary context

   Conservation and variation across homologous proteins.

A modern protein language model can potentially learn several of these patterns from sequence data. However, it does not necessarily recover all structural or functional information perfectly.

# 11. Why are protein sequences so abundant?

Your next slide says:

> Abundant sequence information. Most pLMs rely on protein sequence data because of the sequence–structure knowledge gap.

This contains two important ideas.

## 11.1 Sequence data are abundant

There are very large databases of protein sequences obtained from:

* Genome sequencing

* Metagenomics

* Transcriptomics

* Protein databases

* Computational gene prediction

* Organism sequencing projects

Many sequences have no experimentally verified functional annotation.

This creates a useful situation for self-supervised learning:

The model can learn before being trained for a specific downstream task.

## 11.2 The sequence–structure gap

A protein's primary sequence is one-dimensional:

```
M K T L L V A G ...
```

But a folded protein is a three-dimensional object.

Sequence⟶3D structure⟶Function\text{Sequence} \longrightarrow \text{3D structure} \longrightarrow \text{Function}Sequence⟶3D structure⟶Function

The mapping from sequence to structure and function is complex.

A protein language model trained only on sequences does not receive explicit 3D coordinates for every training example. Nevertheless, sequence patterns are constrained by structural and functional requirements.

For example:

* Hydrophobic residues often contribute to protein cores or membrane regions.

* Conserved residues may be important for function.

* Certain sequence patterns are associated with secondary structures.

* Mutations that disrupt folding may be less tolerated evolutionarily.

The model can learn statistical patterns that reflect some of these constraints.

### Important limitation

Sequence-only pretraining does not guarantee complete structural understanding.

A protein language model may learn useful structural information indirectly, but it is not equivalent to directly training on experimentally measured structures or physical simulations.

# 12. What does “sequence–structure knowledge gap” mean?

The phrase can be understood in two ways.

## 12.1 The biological gap

We have a sequence, but we want to know its structure and function.

For example:

```
Unknown protein sequence
        ↓
What does it fold into?
        ↓
Where does it localize?
        ↓
What does it bind?
        ↓
What does it do?
```

Experimental structure determination and functional characterization are expensive and incomplete.

## 12.2 The machine learning gap

A model may learn sequence patterns, but we need to determine how much structural information those representations contain.

For example, we can test a protein embedding on:

* Secondary-structure prediction

* Contact prediction

* Solvent accessibility

* Subcellular localization

* Protein family classification

* Enzyme function prediction

If an embedding performs well on these tasks, that suggests it contains useful information. It does not prove that the representation has learned a complete physical model of the protein.

# 14. What is a language model?

# 15. N-gram language models

An n-gram model is an early and relatively simple language-modeling approach.

It predicts the next token using a fixed number of preceding tokens.

## 15.1 Bigram model

A bigram model uses one previous token:

P(xt∣xt−1)P(x_t\mid x_{t-1})P(xt∣xt−1)

For example:

```
The → cat
```

It estimates the probability of `cat` given `The`.

## 15.2 Trigram model

A trigram model uses two previous tokens:

P(xt∣xt−2,xt−1)P(x_t\mid x_{t-2},x_{t-1})P(xt∣xt−2,xt−1)

For example:

```
The cat → drinks
```

The model predicts `drinks` using the preceding two words.

## 15.3 General n-gram model

An n-gram model approximates:

P(xt∣x1,…,xt−1)≈P(xt∣xt−n+1,…,xt−1)P(x_t\mid x_1,\ldots,x_{t-1}) \approx P(x_t\mid x_{t-n+1},\ldots,x_{t-1})P(xt∣x1,…,xt−1)≈P(xt∣xt−n+1,…,xt−1)

It assumes that only the previous n−1n-1n−1 tokens matter.

This is called a Markov assumption.

### Example

A 3-gram language model for protein sequences could estimate:

P(at∣at−2,at−1)P(a_t\mid a_{t-2},a_{t-1})P(at∣at−2,at−1)

where ata_tat is the next amino acid.

It might learn that certain residues are more probable after particular local patterns.

# 16. How does an n-gram model work for proteins?

Consider:


P(L∣M,K)P(L\mid M,K)P(L∣M,K)

For example, if a particular amino acid frequently follows a particular local pattern, the estimated probability increases.

## 16.1 Advantages

Traditional n-gram models are:

* Simple to understand

* Relatively easy to train

* Computationally manageable for small contexts

* Useful as historical baselines

## 16.2 Limitations

They have several important limitations:

### A. Fixed context length

A trigram model only considers a short context.

It cannot directly model dependencies between residues that are hundreds of positions apart.

### B. Data sparsity

There may be many possible sequence patterns, but some patterns occur rarely.

The model may encounter combinations that were not present during training.

### C. Large vocabulary problems

If the tokens are 3-grams, the theoretical vocabulary size is:

203=800020^3=8000203=8000

For 6-grams:

206=64,000,00020^6=64,000,000206=64,000,000

This quickly becomes expensive and sparse.

### D. Weak generalization

A traditional lookup-based n-gram model may struggle to generalize between similar but unseen patterns.

Neural embeddings can improve generalization by representing related tokens with similar vectors.

# 18. What is a distributed representation?

A distributed representation stores information across multiple dimensions.

Suppose we have a 4-dimensional representation:

vALV=\(0.8,−0.1,0.5,0.2\)\mathbf{v}_{ALV} = \(0.8,-0.1,0.5,0.2\)vALV=\(0.8,−0.1,0.5,0.2\)

We should not assume:

* Dimension 1 = hydrophobicity

* Dimension 2 = charge

* Dimension 3 = flexibility

* Dimension 4 = structure

Unless the model was explicitly designed and validated that way.

Instead, information may be distributed across several dimensions.

Two related 3-grams might have similar vectors:

vALV≈vLVA\mathbf{v}_{ALV} \approx \mathbf{v}_{LVA}vALV≈vLVA

This can allow downstream models to generalize across related sequence patterns.

The learned vector space is often called an embedding space.

# 19. From n-gram models to neural protein language models

The historical progression is approximately:

1. Statistical n-gram models

   Learn local conditional probabilities from observed sequence patterns.

2. Word2Vec / ProtVec

   Learn dense vectors using context-prediction objectives.

3. Neural sequence models

   Use neural networks such as recurrent networks to process sequences and model broader dependencies.

4. Contextualized protein language models

   Produce representations that depend on the surrounding sequence.

5. Transformer-based pLMs

   Use attention mechanisms to model relationships across sequence positions and learn representations from very large protein datasets.

Examples of modern protein language model families include ESM and ProtT5.

These models may use different training objectives:

* Autoregressive next-token prediction

* Masked-token prediction

* Other self-supervised objectives

# 20. How does masked language modeling work?

Many modern protein language models use masked language modeling.

Suppose the original sequence is:

```
M K T L L V A G T A A
```

We mask a residue:

```
M K T L _ V A G T A A
```

The model must predict the missing amino acid.

P(xi∣x1,…,xi−1,xi+1,…,xT)P(x_i\mid x_1,\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_T)P(xi∣x1,…,xi−1,xi+1,…,xT)

Unlike a strictly left-to-right model, a masked language model can use information from both sides of the masked position.

For example:

```
M K T L [MASK] V A G
```

The model can use:

* Residues before the mask

* Residues after the mask

* Other information from the sequence

The training objective is typically cross-entropy:

L=−∑i∈Mlog⁡P(xi∣x∖M)\mathcal{L} = -\sum_{i\in M}\log P(x_i\mid x_{\setminus M})L=−i∈M∑logP(xi∣x∖M)

where MMM is the set of masked positions.

* The entire embedding matrix

For a downstream kinase-association task, the model might use the representation to learn patterns associated with kinase recognition.

However, a pLM embedding alone does not guarantee that the model knows the correct kinase. The downstream task still requires appropriate training, evaluation, and biological validation.

## 22.1 NLF encoding

NLF is a traditional, handcrafted representation based on amino acid properties and a nonlinear transformation.

It does not dynamically change according to the entire sequence in the same way as a contextualized pLM.

## 22.2 ProtVec

ProtVec learns embeddings for short 3-grams from sequence context.

It is more flexible than a simple one-hot encoding because related 3-grams can receive related vectors.

However, the basic embedding is associated with the 3-gram identity rather than the full context of each occurrence.

## 22.3 Protein language models

A pLM can produce representations based on the surrounding sequence.

For example, the representation of a residue may be affected by other residues in the phosphosite window.

This is useful when the biological meaning of a residue depends on its context.

# 23. Why sequence-only models still have limitations

Even a powerful pLM has limitations.

### 1. A short sequence may not contain enough information

DARKIN uses a short phosphosite-centered window. The sequence may not include:

* The complete kinase-binding region

* Distant structural contacts

* Protein-level localization

* Cellular conditions

* Competing kinases

* Regulatory context

### 2. Biological labels may be incomplete

If an experimentally observed kinase–substrate relationship is absent from a database, the absence does not necessarily mean that the relationship is biologically false.

### 3. Statistical patterns can be shortcuts

A model may learn correlations that work on a benchmark but do not generalize to new proteins or experimental conditions.

This connects to the research ideas in Taştan’s work: evaluating whether a model learns meaningful biological relationships rather than exploiting repeated identities or dataset artifacts.

### 4. Protein sequence is not the entire biological system

Protein function may depend on:

* Protein concentration

* Cell type

* Subcellular location

* Post-translational modifications

* Protein interactions

* Ligand availability

* Environmental conditions

Sequence embeddings are valuable, but they represent only part of the biological information.

# 25. Interview-ready explanation

If Professor Taştan asks you, “Why are protein language models useful?”, you could answer:

> Protein language models learn representations from large collections of unlabeled protein sequences using self-supervised objectives, such as masked amino acid prediction. The intuition is similar to language modeling: sequence context contains statistical information related to biological constraints. Earlier approaches such as ProtVec applied Word2Vec-style skip-gram training to protein 3-grams, producing static 100-dimensional embeddings. Modern protein language models produce contextualized representations, meaning that the representation of an amino acid can depend on its surrounding sequence. This is important because the biological role of a residue depends on its local and long-range context. These embeddings can then be transferred to downstream tasks such as protein-function prediction, kinase–substrate association, and structural prediction.

## Very short version

> ProtVec treats protein 3-grams like words and learns a fixed vector for each 3-gram using skip-gram. Modern protein language models go further by generating contextualized representations, where the embedding of a residue depends on the surrounding protein sequence. This helps capture sequence patterns that may be related to protein structure and function.
